# Counsel Questions and Drafting Notes

**Working memo for Timeless Way Interactive, LLC — not part of any executed instrument.**

These notes were previously embedded inside the Operating Agreement, the Individual
Contributor Agreement, and the Member Perpetual License. They have been moved here for a
reason worth stating plainly: several of them record open legal risk in the Company's own
words — worker classification, wage-law exposure on volunteer engagements, unresolved tax
characterization. Inside a signed instrument, that language is an admission attached to the
document an adversary reads first. It belongs in a working memo instead, where it can be
worked through with counsel and a CPA and retired as each question is answered.

Nothing here is binding, and nothing here changes the meaning of the instruments. Each note
is listed with the document and section it came from.

## Operating Agreement

### DRAFTING NOTE — Collective Seat: what your CPA must reconcile

*From: 3.4 The Collective Seat*

Internally, the Pool is computed as if it were one Member. But for tax and accounting, payments to Contractors are 1099 compensation — a deductible Company expense — while Member distributions are partnership allocations. Expenses reduce Surplus, and Surplus determines the size of every Seat’s share, so a naive reading makes the math circular.

The intended resolution: compute the per-Seat split on Surplus before deducting Collective Seat compensation, pay the Pool its computed share as compensation, and let the deduction fall where the accountant puts it. Confirm the exact mechanics — and the effect on each Member’s K-1 — with the CPA before the first Pool contractor is admitted.

Also confirm worker-classification risk: a long-running revenue-share contractor working alongside Members can start to look like an employee to the IRS and TWC. The candidacy path in Section 3.3 is the intended answer (they become owners), but get advice on timing.

### DRAFTING NOTE — Shared Member Seat: tax ordering

*From: 3.5 Shared Member Seat Arrangements*

Two questions for the CPA. (1) Are payments to a sponsored Contractor a Company expense that reduces the Sponsoring Member’s allocated Surplus (cleaner: less phantom income for the sponsor), or a redirection of the sponsor’s distribution (sponsor is taxed on the full share)? The answer changes the sponsor’s Section 8.4 tax floor. (2) This Agreement assumes the first characterization: the sponsor’s mandatory minimum under 8.4 is computed on their share net of sponsored-Contractor payments. Confirm.

### DRAFTING NOTE — self-assigned titles vs. roles the law requires

*From: 5.5 Self-Direction and Self-Assigned Roles*

Self-assigned creative titles are fine and live in the handbook. Separately, banks, tax filings, and counterparties will need at least one or two people with authority to sign on the Company’s behalf. Article 6 creates a minimal Secretary and Treasurer for that purpose. These statutory roles are administrative plumbing, not a hierarchy; keep them distinct from the creative titles Members choose for themselves.

### DRAFTING NOTE — why the mandatory cash floor exists

*From: 8.4 Distributions and Mandatory Minimum Cash*

Repeating the key point from the Preamble because it lives operationally here: without this floor, a Member who took no cash could still owe income tax on their full allocated share and have no money to pay it. The floor guarantees every Member at least enough cash to cover the tax. Set the percentage with your CPA — 20% is a placeholder; effective rates vary with each Member’s total income and state of residence.

### DRAFTING NOTE — why this separation model

*From: 8.7 Payout on Separation*

This is the recommended approach over an “equity buy-back at fair market value.” Appraising an indie studio’s equity is genuinely hard and a reliable source of disputes — there is rarely a clean number, and a departing Member and the remaining group will rarely agree on it. Converting the Member’s accrued economic balance into a fixed debt paid over time avoids the valuation fight entirely, gives the leaver a predictable payout, and protects the Company’s cash flow with the solvency escape hatch in clause 3. It pairs naturally with the no-buy-in, equal-split structure you chose.

It also separates cleanly from IP: the leaver keeps their Section 7.3 license (they take the work with them) and gets their economic balance paid out. The two are independent, which is what you want.

### DRAFTING NOTE — entity tax classification is not yet settled here

*From: 10.3 Tax Reporting*

This Agreement is drafted assuming the default: a multi-member LLC taxed as a partnership (Schedule K-1 to each Member). While you are solo, the LLC is instead a disregarded entity (you report on Schedule C) and most of Articles 4–9 are dormant. The open question for your CPA is whether to (a) stay a partnership-taxed LLC with equal allocations, or (b) elect Subchapter T cooperative treatment — which would require hours-based patronage allocation and is in tension with your equal-split choice (see the note at Section 8.3). Resolve this before you admit your second Member, because it changes how 8.3 and 8.4 are written.

## Individual Contributor Agreement

### NOTE — when Option D is appropriate

*From: *☐  Option D — Volunteer engagement (no compensation)**

Use Option D only where payment is not a viable path for the Contributor (Section 1 above) and the work is genuinely self-directed. Under wage law, a “volunteer” whose work is sustained or production-critical may be owed minimum wage regardless of this Agreement — a third party’s non-compete does not waive wage rights against this Company. Move any such contributor to a revenue-share option (A or B) with a real Threshold. See the drafting note at Section 3.8 of the Operating Agreement.

### NOTE — why full assignment

*From: 5. Intellectual Property*

Section 3.7 of the Operating Agreement requires this: the Company cannot license or protect work it does not own, and no project should enter the Work Pool encumbered by retained contractor rights. The intended long-term path is not for contributors to retain slivers of IP — it is for them to become Members, at which point they receive the same perpetual, irrevocable license to all Company IP that every Member holds (Section 7.3 of the Operating Agreement).

## Member Perpetual License

### DRAFTING NOTE — tell publishers early

*From: 7. Non-Exclusivity; Later Company Licenses*

A publisher negotiating an “exclusive” license will discover in diligence that every current and former Member holds a perpetual, irrevocable license to the same IP. That is by design — it is the cooperative’s core bargain — but it must be disclosed early, priced into the deal, and drafted around (for example, exclusivity as against everyone except Members exercising personal licenses). Raise it in the first conversation, not the last.

## Open items added by the adversarial review

These are not from the original notes; they are questions the review surfaced that only
counsel, a CPA, or a filed document can answer.

### Is the Certificate of Formation consistent with member management?

The Operating Agreement assumes the Members govern (Article 5). Texas asks on the
certificate whether the LLC is managed by managers or by members. If a manager was named at
filing, the certificate and Article 5 disagree, and the certificate is the public document.
Pull the filed certificate and confirm. While you are in it, add the Section 9.4 borrowing
and encumbrance restrictions to the certificate, so a lender cannot claim it had no notice.

### Does the no-borrowing rule actually bind a third party?

Section 9.4 says a prohibited obligation is "void as against the Company." Against a
counterparty without notice, a member of a member-managed Texas LLC can still carry apparent
authority. The review added a personal indemnity, an indemnification carve-out, and a duty to
give written notice in credit-adjacent agreements. Ask counsel whether anything further is
available in Texas — certificate language, a recorded statement, or a standard rider.

### Section 8.7 remains the softest point in the debt armor

The separation payout is a real debt to a real creditor who happens to be a former colleague.
The review made it unsecured and barred it from reaching Company IP, and paused interest
during dormancy. It did not make it contingent on Surplus, because doing so would weaken the
leaver's claim. That is a deliberate trade: the Work Pool is protected from execution, but a
former Member with an unpaid judgment is still a creditor with the ordinary remedies of one.
Confirm with counsel that the anti-execution language holds up, and decide as a group whether
you want to go further.

### Volunteer engagements under Option D

The existing note is right and probably understates it: a for-profit company generally cannot
accept volunteer labor for its own benefit under the FLSA, and a third party's non-compete
does not change that. The review added consideration (a license back to the volunteer's own
work), narrowed the "other reason" blank, and put a thirty-day clock on converting to paid
work. None of that makes the classification safe. Get advice before the first Option D
engagement, not after.

### Deferred, unpaid balances and wage law

An Unpaid Balance is deliberately not a debt. Ask counsel how that characterization holds up
against a Texas Payday Act or FLSA claim by a contributor who worked for years and was paid
little, and whether the reclassification credit now in Section 4.5 of the ICA does the work
intended.

### Does declaring "no employees" actually prevent employee status?

Section 3.2 now states the Company has no employees and runs no payroll, and Section 8.8
bars wages to Members. That is a real structural commitment — no payroll genuinely is what
makes the slowdown posture in Section 9.3 work — but a declaration in a governing document
does not bind the IRS, the Texas Workforce Commission, or a court. Those bodies apply their
own control and economic-reality tests to the facts. Two questions for counsel: whether
anything in the way contributors actually work would flunk those tests, and whether the
explicit disclaimer helps or hurts if it ever comes up (it can cut both ways — evidence of
intent, but also evidence the parties knew the question was live).

### The six-month admit-or-end ceiling: does it help or hurt classification?

Sections 2.3, 3.3 and ICA Section 2.6 now end any engagement at six months unless the
contributor is admitted as a Member or has declined membership in writing. The worker-side
case for this is strong and it is what the collective asked for: it makes a permanent
non-owner class structurally impossible.

Two things to check. First, a fixed six-month relationship with an integration-then-admit
path may read to an agency as more employment-like, not less — the length of the
relationship is one of the classification factors. Second, confirm the exception is drafted
tightly enough: it can only be opened by the contributor's own written declination, and it
renews only by Supermajority with the reason minuted. If either of those slips in practice,
the ceiling becomes decorative.

Also worth deciding as a group: what happens to a genuinely short specialist engagement — a
composer hired for one track — that runs slightly over six months. Today the answer is
"they decline membership in writing and the Members renew." Confirm that is the answer you
want.

### The Reserve Percentage placeholder

Section 8.3 now fixes the reserve at a stated percentage of Surplus, changeable only by
unanimous written consent. **Twenty percent (20%) is a placeholder, not advice.** Set the
real figure with the CPA before execution, against the Company's actual revenue shape:
game revenue arrives in lumps years after the work, so the reserve is what carries the
Company between them. Too low and a lean year forces dormancy; too high and the Collective
Seat never pays out and the ceiling in Section 2.3 pushes contributors out before they see
any money. Model both before you pick a number that then takes unanimity to move.

### Partnership tax mechanics

Article 8 allocates equally without regard to contribution, and the review added a
no-deficit-restoration statement. A partnership tax adviser should confirm whether the
allocations need Section 704(b) substantial-economic-effect language or a qualified income
offset, or whether the Company will simply file consistently and accept the risk.
